A 350-year-old idea, and why it is worth another look.

A vacuum displaces air with nothing at all, which is the lightest lift there is. Francesco Lana de Terzi worked that out in 1670 and it has never flown, because a shell thin enough to float has always been too thin to survive the atmosphere pressing in on it. What changed is not a stronger material. It is a better arrangement of ordinary ones, and it moves the problem from a geometry failure nobody can engineer around to a strength number you can look up and improve.

The copied science catalogue reports 108 sources; this site repository does not include the catalogue needed to recount them. It marks 10 as contradicting the project. This page discusses nine selected objections, with access and review status recorded beside each source.

The objections were compiled on 9 August 2026; the catalogue count reflects the current science repository.

A 350-year-old problem

Settled physics

1670

Lana de Terzi proposes it, and is immediately right about everything except the metal

Francesco Lana de Terzi's Prodromo describes an airship lifted by four evacuated copper spheres. The insight is exact. A vacuum beats any gas, because a gas still weighs something. The failure is also exact, and Lana himself saw it: thin the sphere enough to float and the atmosphere crushes it.

The trap

Buckling, not strength, is what killed it for three centuries

Squeeze a structure and there are two ways it can die. It can crush, when the material gives up under load. Or it can buckle, when the shape snaps, the way an empty can folds under a foot. Buckling is the treacherous one. It arrives while the material is nowhere near its limits, and it takes the whole shape at once. For a thin sphere holding out one atmosphere it arrives very early indeed.

Akhmeteli and Gavrilin state the consequence precisely. Floating and surviving together demand a material with E/ρ² above about 4.5 × 10⁵ in SI units. Diamond reaches 1 × 10⁵. A homogeneous single-layer evacuated sphere is impossible for every known solid, diamond included: the paper's finding covers “any solid material in existence”, not every material that might one day be made. That is why the answer had to be a different architecture rather than a better substance.

What changed

Demonstrated at small scale

Ordinary cellular solids, including foams and honeycombs, lose stiffness faster than they lose mass: halve the density and you lose rather more than half the modulus. That quadratic-or-worse scaling is why light structures have always been floppy structures.

Architected lattices break the scaling. A stretch-dominated microlattice keeps stiffness roughly linear in relative density over three orders of magnitude. Discrete lattices are assembled from many identical mass-produced parts rather than formed as one piece, and they carry that property into structures big enough to fly. That is the whole of the change: the arrangement, not the material.

Jenett, Gregg and Cheung apply it to the vacuum balloon directly, and their result is the one this project is built on: with a lattice filling the volume rather than a skin wrapping it, the binding constraint moves from stability to strength. Strength per kilogram is a number that can be looked up, tested on a bench, and bought more of. A buckle is a whole-shape failure. A strength failure is designed to stay local: one strut, with the load meant to redistribute around it. That behaviour is still to be demonstrated at this scale.

Supporting: Cheung & Gershenfeld, Reversibly Assembled Cellular Composite Materials (2013); Gregg et al., Ultra-Light and Scalable Composite Lattice Materials (2018); Zheng et al., Ultralight, Ultrastiff Mechanical Metamaterials (2014); Berger et al., on lattices at the theoretical isotropic stiffness limit (2017).

The nine hardest counter-arguments

Two found and corrected the same day

A catalogue that only holds the papers agreeing with you is a bibliography for a press release. These nine objections include locally captured papers and catalogue-only sources. Two bear directly on the dry-mass budget, the number the concept turns on.

Addendum, August 2026: these objections addressed the earlier cell-array ledger and reference sphere. The current candidate models film on rings over a two-walled skeleton. Structural mass remains open.

Structure

The dry-mass budget fails against the project's own cited precedent

The ledger assumes the entire dry mass of a ship equals the water it carries. For the largest class, 10,000 tonnes inside 22 million cubic metres, or 0.455 kg per cubic metre of hull. Jenett's Table 2 gives a bare lattice shell at 0.508 kg/m³, at every radius. That is twelve per cent over the entire dry allowance before a skin, a joint, a rotor, a tank or a battery is fitted.

Jenett, Gregg & Cheung, Discrete Lattice Material Vacuum Airship, AIAA SciTech 2019 · hosted PDF · NASA NTRS

Structure

Structure alone consumes the whole budget in the only real-materials design

Metlen and Palazotto define W/B, the ratio of structure weight to displaced-air weight, and optimise three vacuum designs against it. Their best real-materials result is W/B = 0.81, and their non-exotic case reaches 0.94. Structure alone eats what we allocate to structure and payload together.

Metlen & Palazotto, AFIT, 2013 · paywalled, catalogued by DOI, not hosted

Method

The scale invariance both structural sources assume cannot hold

Both papers hold shell mass fraction constant with radius. That assumption is what lets a metre-scale demonstration imply a 219-metre hull. Derveni and colleagues show that in pressurised shells with distributed defects, the single most severe defect sets the buckling knockdown factor. Removing every lesser defect changes it by under 4 per cent. Defect count is not invariant with radius. It grows with the number of manufactured features, and so does the expected severity of the worst one.

Derveni et al., 2024 · paywalled, catalogued by DOI

Energy

The nitrogen store returned more work than the nitrogen contained

An early model recovered 225 kWh from a tonne of liquid nitrogen whose physical exergy against a 288 K ambient is 173.4 kWh. That is a thermodynamic violation, found by going back to the source. It is 90 kWh/t now, and a test fails the build if the number ever climbs back over the exergy.

Arnaiz-del-Pozo et al., 2020, nitrogen liquefaction exergy · catalogued, open access

Energy

The solar skin needed 76% conversion efficiency

NRCan's dataset gives 6.34 kWh/m²/day mean July horizontal insolation across eight interior fire-belt towns, or 264 W/m² incident. The earlier model credited 200 W/m² of electricity. Its August correction used 45 W/m², and the then-published endurance figures fell. The largest class changed from 61.1 hours to 36.3, the smallest from 35.4 to 10.4.

Changing the vehicle's power supply by a factor of 4.4 moved no published figure at all: not throughput, not energy per tonne, not one number on the fleet monitor. Generation is not in the ledger those are computed from. A model can be badly wrong about something central and have no test anywhere able to notice.

Natural Resources Canada, photovoltaic potential and solar resource maps

Batteries

The largest class's battery can exceed its dry allowance

The configured P-100, P-1000 and P-10000 carry 0.20, 0.12, 0.20 MWh of battery per tonne of dry allowance, respectively. Pack mass must be costed at a stated installed specific energy. NASA's X-57 turned 225 Wh/kg cells into a 149 Wh/kg pack, and that factor worsens as cells improve because thermal-runaway containment mass grows with the energy it contains. At 149 Wh/kg our largest class's 2,000 MWh would mass about 13,400 tonnes against its 10,000-tonne dry allowance.

Chin et al., Battery Cell-to-Pack Scaling Trends for Electric Aircraft, NASA NTRS 2021 · Lvovich, Energy Storage for NASA Missions, 2020

Effectiveness

The drop may dissipate before it lands

The Forest Service's 2022 assessment states that a drop released 1,000 feet above ground or vegetation “would completely dissipate”. Our release altitude is 450 metres (1,476 feet), set for hull clearance but never costed against delivery. The model has no droplet physics at all: it moves tonnes from a tank to a coordinate.

USDA Forest Service, nationwide aerial application of fire retardant, 2022

Effectiveness

Tonnes is not what the field measures

AFUE observed 27,611 drops at 272 incidents across 18 states. Overall probability of success is 0.82, but for airtankers used to halt fire spread it falls to 0.55–0.67, and without ground engagement it is 0.56 against 0.72 with it. The modal outcome for a drop with no ground crew is not effective. AFUE never counts tonnes. Tonnes per hour is what the model computes; reconciling it with what the field measures is an open strategic question, and the pre-ignition doctrine is the answer the project is testing.

USDA Forest Service, Aerial Firefighting Use and Effectiveness, 2020

Operations

Rotor wash spreads fire, and the fleet's is unmodelled

Helicopter downwash becomes a surface wind that can spread fire along the ground, and a hovering Black Hawk must be well over 160 feet up before its wash falls below 30 mph at the surface. The largest class runs about 79 kg/m² of disc loading against the Black Hawk's roughly 47. Its fourteen rotors have a combined disc area equal to a single 451-metre disc. None of its effect on the fire, or on the lake it hovers over, is modelled.

Suter, Rotor Wash, 2005

Where the vacuum-lift literature actually stands

Two for, two against

It is a small literature and it does not agree with itself. Read in one sitting, it says the idea is not impossible and is not demonstrated, and that the margin between those two states is narrow.

Jenett 2019NASA Ames and MIT. A discrete lattice moves the constraint from buckling to strength, and initial design studies show viable designs “with large margins for skin mass penalties”. Our load-bearing source. Its Table 2 breaks our mass budget.
Akhmeteli & Gavrilin 2021An independent route: a continuous sandwich shell rather than a lattice. Concludes a rigid vacuum balloon can theoretically be built from commercially available materials, at a payload fraction of 0.1 against the 0.5 our ledger implies.
Metlen & Palazotto 2013The most careful structural optimisation in the set, and the least encouraging: W/B of 0.81 with exotic materials, 0.94 without.
Derveni 2024Not about vacuum airships at all, which is what makes it the strongest of the nine: it undermines the scale invariance both positive results assume, without having any stake in the argument.

Also catalogued: Graves (2019) on a celestial-icosahedron frame; Snyder & Palazotto (2018) on a hexakis icosahedron; Hilburger (2023) for the current NASA buckling design recommendations; and Toli (2026) on a geodesic vacuum-buoyant vehicle, which could not be obtained in full and is therefore not relied on. The catalogue says so in its own entry.

Airship engineering, from people who have built them

Supporting

The vacuum question is exotic. Almost everything else about the vehicle is not. Buoyant flight has a century of engineering behind it and a conventional literature that a new concept has no excuse for ignoring.

Ardema (NASA, 1984) surveys propelled lighter-than-air concepts and their missions. Its sizing, drag and mass relationships give a baseline for this study. Colozza & Dolce (2003) size a solar-powered stratospheric airship. They work through the coupling that dominates this ledger: hull volume against station-keeping drag power against array area against storage mass. Hochstetler (2016) sets out what a heavy-lift airship simulation has to model to be credible. That includes the buoyancy-management and ballast-exchange problems that arrive the moment a lighter-than-air vehicle puts something down.

That last one is the oldest problem in cargo airships and it is ours too: unload a thousand tonnes and you are a thousand tonnes buoyant. Our answer to it is in the next section.

What the fire community measures

Contradicts us

The project publishes tonnes per hour because it is what the model computes. The field publishes something else, and the gap between the two is a strategic risk, not an arithmetic one.

AFUE is the largest field study of aerial firefighting effectiveness there is, at 27,611 observed drops. It scores whether a drop achieved its own tactical objective. It never counts tonnes, never counts fires extinguished, and reports that the single strongest determinant of success is whether ground crews were engaged with the drop. An uncrewed fleet delivering enormous tonnage without ground engagement is, on the only large study available, operating in the least effective mode that study measured.

That finding is why direct attack is the demonstration rather than the mission. The proposed pre-ignition doctrine covers corridor wetting, fuel-break pre-wetting, and soil and fuel moisture ahead of forecast fire weather. That work would not require a ground crew under a drop, and it is set out in full on the how-it-works page.

The precedent for the one mechanism below is also from this field. The collapsible suspended bucket was invented by Don Arney in 1978, first delivered in 1982 and in production from 1983, in capacities from 270 litres to 9,800 litres for heavy-lift helicopters such as the CH-47. The largest ship's bag is 12,400 tonnes: 1,265 times the largest one ever built. The precedent establishes that the mechanism works. It establishes nothing about the engineering at this scale, and the page says so wherever the number appears.

Water access requires a source-specific legal and hydrological review. The current model selects sources by geometry; it does not establish permission to divert water.

One mechanism not found in the literature

Published deliberately

In the fleet model, a hull sized for its loaded lift is difficult to push down when empty. The hardest point is over the lake, where air is densest. The largest class models 13,723 tonnes of surplus lift there against 12,666 tonnes of rotor authority. It cannot reach its modelled water source under rotor power alone. The proposed answer is to borrow the lake.

The proposed ship would lower a cable and fill a collapsible bag at the surface. In the earlier flight model, twelve thousand tonnes on the cable supplies downward force. The bag would be raised fifteen metres and emptied back into the lake. Rotor power rises as thrust1.5, so the concept sizes the bag for the whole descent. Its earlier-model estimate is 0.6 MWh spent and 34.5 MWh of rotor work avoided; those figures are under review.

The principle is old. Nineteenth-century balloonists used trail ropes to transfer weight to the ground during descent and lift it back on ascent. The dip-and-fill half resembles the Bambi bucket, in service since 1983. This proposal combines a suspended water bag with rotor thrust to manage the modelled descent; its full engineering and energy bill remain open.

Why this is published. The project does not plan to patent this proposal. Publication makes the arithmetic open for scrutiny. Its effect on patent rights requires a legal review.

Three things the descent anchor still owes the ledger: the pendulum dynamics of a twelve-thousand-tonne bag on one cable under an 876-metre hull; the 440 mm of UHMWPE cable it needs, about 125 tonnes, sized but not yet charged to the mass budget; and the rotor wash it hovers in, which is the last entry in the section above.
