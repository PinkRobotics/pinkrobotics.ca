We intend to fight wildfires with overwhelming water, delivered by giant airships that never need to land.

Autonomous vacuum airships that drink whole payloads straight from a lake through a hanging hose, carry a hundred to ten thousand tonnes at a time, and cycle water onto a fire line around the clock. No such aircraft exists yet, ours or anyone's, so we are doing the next most convincing thing: running the entire concept in public, against today's real BC fires, with every assumption a visible dial.

Sixty seconds of reading below is enough to understand the whole idea. Everything past that (the physics case, the energy budget, the honest limits) is on this page and the how-it-works page, worked through in the open. A second project, partnerships with working animals, is deliberately parked while the airships carry the focus.

The idea, in sixty seconds

Renders of the reference concept

Wildfire ultimately loses to one thing: more water than it can boil away. Aircraft today deliver it in single-digit tonnes per drop, minutes of water spread across hours of flying. This is a machine designed around the other end of that arithmetic. Every image below is a render of the working reference model, not an artist's impression.

The one-sentence status, always true on this page: no flightworthy vacuum airship exists; these are designs and public arithmetic, not aircraft. What makes this more than a poster is that the arithmetic is running, live, where anyone can check it.

What the company is

Software running

Pink Robotics is the parent company. Pink AI sits inside it and builds the software: Helm, a substrate for AI systems that keep a durable memory and stay accountable for what they do with it, and Maeve, the first character running on it. That half is built and running, and its ledgers are published. Pink Robotics is the layer where a system like that acts on the world, under one rule that crosses everything it will ever build: a generative mind may propose, but deterministic systems decide what physical commands are permitted.

The physical layer is the same problem, with the safety margins removed.

A machine that can act has to be able to explain what it did and to stop when it is unsure. Getting that right in software is a research problem. Getting it right with a hundred tonnes of water at altitude, or one day around a living animal, is the same problem with physical consequences.

Airships

Proposed

Lift comes from displacing air with something less dense. Helium works and is scarce. Hydrogen works and burns. A vacuum is the limit case: nothing at all inside.

A cell that flies can carry water. Sized up, one vehicle lifts a hundred tonnes of it; built out, a million such vehicles could in theory deliver an atmospheric river, the only measure there is for water at that scale. Pointed at wildfire, at drought, at wherever water is needed; drawn from where it is best spared; moved without carrying one watershed's problems into another.

No flightworthy vacuum airship has been demonstrated by anyone, which is why everything starts at a single cell.

NASA and MIT research concludes viable vacuum airships are possible, and independent analysis agrees.

The vacuum airship has been studied seriously, recently, and from two directions. The closest work to what this page proposes is from NASA Ames and MIT: a vacuum airship built as a discrete lattice, assembled from mass-produced building-block parts rather than formed as one continuous shell.

Through the use of high performance building block elements, modular, scalable and extensible aircraft can be rapidly assembled into positive net-buoyancy systems utilizing a vacuum instead of a lifting gas.
By applying cellular material scaling laws and taking advantage of developments in architected materials, we show that lattice materials can be used to overcome traditional stability challenges.
Initial design studies conducted with cuboctahedral discrete lattice materials show that viable designs are possible, with large margins for skin mass penalties.

Jenett, B.E. (MIT); Gregg, C.E.; Cheung, K.C. (NASA Ames Research Center). “Discrete Lattice Material Vacuum Airship.” AIAA SciTech Forum, 2019. doi:10.2514/6.2019-0815 · NASA NTRS · hosted copy, PDF. Their key structural finding: lattice vacuum balloons are strength-limited rather than buckling-limited, which retires the failure mode that sank the idea for three centuries.

What strength-limited instead of buckling-limited means, in plain terms.

Squeeze a structure and there are two ways it can die. It can crush: the material itself gives up when the load beats its strength. Or it can buckle: the shape snaps, the way an empty can folds under a foot or a ping-pong ball pops inward. Buckling is the treacherous one, because it happens while the material is still nowhere near its limits. The geometry fails, not the stuff, and it fails all at once, everywhere. For a thin sphere holding out one atmosphere it arrives early: any skin light enough to float is far too thin to stay round. That is the exact trap that made the vacuum balloon impossible for three hundred years, no matter what the skin was made of.

The NASA and MIT result is that a lattice filling the volume, rather than a skin wrapping it, steps out of the trap. The lattice's own geometry holds the shape, so what limits the design is now the material's strength per kilogram: a number you can look up, test on a bench, and buy more of. And where a buckle is a whole-shape event, a strength failure is local, one strut, and the load flows around it. That property, failure that stays small, is the same reasoning behind the sponge structure described below.

An independent route reaches the same verdict. Akhmeteli and Gavrilin, writing in the journal Eng in 2021, analyse a continuous sandwich shell instead of a lattice, and conclude:

We showed that a lighter-than-air rigid vacuum balloon can theoretically be built using commercially available materials.

Akhmeteli, A.; Gavrilin, A.V. “Vacuum Balloon–A 350-Year-Old Dream.” Eng 2021, 2(4), 480–491. doi:10.3390/eng2040030 · publisher · hosted copy, PDF · open access, CC BY 4.0.

Two teams, two structural philosophies, one conclusion. Both are studies, not vehicles, and the ledger above budgets far more aggressively than either, which is honest about how much harder a water carrier is than an existence proof.

The likely structure is a sponge, not an egg — printed in sections.

The working suspicion is that the cell will not be a shell at all. A single sealed surface is an egg: one geometry, one buckling story, one way to die. The designs that look most promising instead are volumetric: a sponge of many sealed voids in mixed printed materials: foaming lightweight filaments like PLA Aero, the stock RC aircraft are printed from today; carbon-fibre filament; and 3D-printed metal, each where it earns its place, in an organic cellular geometry that is generated and optimised by software rather than drawn by hand, and that spans scales the way bone does. A structure like that has no single surface whose failure is the whole story: damage stays local, and the list of fatal failure modes gets short.

The intent is to 3D print it in sections, in the latest composite materials and techniques, and sections are also how a seventy-metre vehicle gets built, tested, and replaced one piece at a time. This is the construction philosophy the NASA and MIT lattice work above arrives at, taken further: many small parts, or a printed continuum, instead of one perfect egg.

Weather effects are a research question.

Weather control is not the goal. But delivering water is making rain, to a degree, and a system operating at this scale may turn out to influence weather in earnest. We treat that as a research obligation: understand the effect, use it where it genuinely helps, and above all never make weather worse.

The first milestone is unglamorous and falsifiable.

A complete evacuated cell that is still positively buoyant once its skin, joints, valves, sensors and safety factors are counted, and that fails without cascade. Everything else waits behind it.

The field station

Intent · no land acquired

The airship programme cannot be closed indoors: sealed instrumented cells fit an ordinary shop, but a tethered demonstrator needs a building and a field; the parked animal partnership, when it resumes, needs animals, weather and a winter. The intended next physical step is a working ranch run as a field station. No land has been acquired, optioned, or placed under offer. The search is open. One candidate property in the British Columbia interior has been given a first structured look: seventeen research passes across parcel, groundwater, water-rights, floodplain, soil-survey, zoning and agricultural-reserve records, plus federal climate and airspace data, published at pinkdata.ca. That review is fast and wide rather than deep, and it ages every day; it is one considered option, not a chosen one. Other properties, in other regions, will be considered alongside it.

Where a farm stops being enough is written down.

The site carries the airship project's first two phases: sealed instrumented cells in an ordinary shop, and five- to twenty-metre tethered demonstrators in one building. Free flight would convert the property into an aerodrome-like use requiring experimental-aircraft authorization, and an obviously aerospace building on agricultural-reserve land requires a non-farm-use application. Those are handoffs, not stretch goals.

The animal partnership, parked

Parked · by choice

The animal is a partner, not a platform.

The company's second project pairs a persistent AI with one working animal, for that animal's life, its agency and right to refuse engineered in from the first line, on the Five Domains welfare model. It is parked, not abandoned: the airships carry the company's whole focus for now, and a project this delicate deserves a whole focus of its own. What survives the parking is written down: a twelve-article welfare covenant, a nine-seat council designed to be seated before any animal wears a device, a five-phase roadmap that puts no animal in it for the first six months, and one idea that never parked at all: the split where the mind proposes and a deterministic system decides, which now governs the airship fleet's architecture instead.

What resuming would take.

The field station, running; the airship work far enough along to share attention; and the council seated first. The covenant's articles (stakeholder status, net welfare benefit, non-aversive by enumeration, refusal as information, assent rather than compliance, rest and privacy among them) wait in the repository, versioned, unchanged by the parking. Nothing about the pause loosens them.

What we're not claiming

  • No flightworthy vacuum airship exists — not ours, not anyone's.
  • The public simulation's assumptions and calculations may contain errors or plain bugs. We believe them accurate and publish them so they can be checked, but they are a demonstration of the idea, not engineering analysis.
  • The animal-partnership project is parked: nothing in it is built.
  • No land has been bought, optioned or placed under offer.
  • Nothing here establishes market size, willingness to pay, clinical validity or regulatory standing. No aviation, agricultural or veterinary authority has reviewed these projects.
  • Pink Robotics is one person. There is no team, no hardware, no prototype, no deployment.
