Energy is cargo.

Electricity is generated where grids are strong, packaged into standardized battery modules, and moved by autonomous airships to wherever the fleet is doing work. This page is that chain, priced end to end: six stations, one module standard, and the full bill at BC Hydro's published tariff.

Conceptual energy infrastructure is the label everything below wears, except the real BC grid facts and the simulated fleet deficit table.

The chain

Six stations. Grid to depot to module to shuttle to buffer to working ship. Charged modules flow right, depleted modules flow left along the same chain. The mechanism is conceptual and named. The simulation models the demand it must meet. The chain is scoped separately.

The module

The PEM, Pink Energy Module: one tonne, 300 kWh nameplate, 240 usable. Five make a magazine for rapid exchange. Charged and depleted modules differ by 0.108 g in 9,000 t, so energy state is invisible to lift. The vehicle is designed around an invariant battery payload. The interface is the standard, not the chemistry.

The exchange

Paired and mass-balanced: charged in as depleted out, through opposing ports near the centre of gravity, so the vehicle feels nothing. Eight ports cycling 1.5 MWh magazines at thirty seconds is a 1.44 GW-equivalent module-transfer rate with no gigawatt connector anywhere.

Transfer times, mechanical target versus complete operation.

A full P-E1000 reload is 11.25 minutes of pure transfer: 180 magazines through 8 paired ports at 30 s each. A P-E10000 takes 56.25 minutes through 16 ports, or 28.13 through an optional 32. Rendezvous, capture, verification and separation are counted separately, wherever the figure appears.

The rejected alternative sets the scale: moving a P-E1000's 270 MWh electrically in ten minutes would mean a 1.62 GW airborne connector. Moving modules gets the same equivalent rate through latches.

The depot

The ground half of the chain. Modules circulate through six ground inventories: charged, charging, cooling, inspected, quarantined, reserve. Charging is slow and parallel on purpose, decoupled from airship turnaround, and every pass through the depot is an automatic health inspection. A 400 MW fleet keeps 3.6 GWh circulating.

Inventory arithmetic, and what a depot is comparable to.

Little's Law sizes the loop: 400 MW × 6 h loop × 1.2 reserve / 0.8 usable depth = 3.6 GWh nameplate circulating. A 7 h loop needs 4.2 GWh. Station tiers run from research (1–10 MW) through regional (50–200) to strategic (500 MW–1+ GW). NASA's NEAT electric-aircraft testbed tops out around the 12 MW class, and megawatt road charging at 3.75 MW. A Pink depot is transmission-scale industrial infrastructure rather than a large charging station.

Circulation is also the safety argument for flying thousands of tonnes of lithium. Every ground touch is a screening event, 100% fast multimodal inspection, with CT targeted and triggered rather than every cell every time.

The mass flow the chain cannot hide.

At 1.2 MWh usable per 5 t magazine, delivered power is module tonnage in motion: 40 MW is 167 t/h, 100 MW is 417, 300 MW is 1,250, 500 MW is 2,083, and a gigawatt is 4,167 tonnes per hour, continuously. That mass flow is the number this chain exists to make visible: at gigawatt scale, delivered power is module tonnage in motion.

The bill

Real BC grid facts

Swapping removes aircraft charging delay. It does not remove average grid demand, battery capital, cycle wear, fire safety, inspection, or the physical mass flow of modules.

The chain makes the energy bill payable. It does not make it small. A 400 MW useful fleet needs about 470.6 MW of average grid input before tender flight and station auxiliaries, US$540M to 1.8B of circulating module inventory, and thousands of tonnes of module flow per hour at gigawatt scale. The chain is credible as large infrastructure and not as an inexpensive accessory to the airship.

Real BC Hydro, and what the electricity would cost.

BC Hydro reports more than 98% clean, hydro-dominated generation, with about 13.4 GW of maximum capacity after Site C. Under transmission rate RS 1830 effective 2026-04-01, demand is C$12.178/kVA · month plus 4.914¢/kWh energy. Input electricity runs about C$67.7/MWh at a 90% load factor, and about C$79.6/MWh delivered at 85% chain efficiency. The 400 MW illustration bills about C$22.61M a month, roughly C$77.4/MWh. Arithmetic illustration, not a quote.

Proximity to a transmission line does not establish spare capacity or permission to connect; every depot requires a utility interconnection study.

Capital and wear: battery inventory is likely to dominate station economics.

Module cost scenarios run US$150 / 300 / 500 per kWh; market pack prices of US$108–139/kWh are lower bounds, not Pink quotes. A P-E1000's inventory is $40.5M–135M, a P-E10000's $405M–1.35B, and the 3.6 GWh depot loop $540M–1.8B. Cycle wear at $300/kWh, 5,000 cycles and 0.8 depth is $75 per delivered MWh, in a scenario table spanning $17–625.

What stays aboard

Liquid nitrogen is the mass battery: ballast, cold reservoir, and a partially recoverable store at a 0.20 round trip under a test-enforced exergy ceiling, never the imported-energy carrier. Solar is an endurance extender at the corrected 45 W/m² day-average. Fuel is the emergency and remote fallback, not used in the primary B.C. model.

The nitrogen numbers.

eLN2 = 0.45 kWh/kg, rtLN2 = 0.20, ceiling rtLN2 × eLN2 × 1000 ≤ 173.4 kWh/t, enforced by test. Tanks of 155 / 1,550 / 15,500 t are sized by unpowered recovery. Their full-tank expansion energy of 13.95 / 139.5 / 1,395 MWh is 0.70× / 1.16× / 0.70× of each class's battery. The store is the same order as the battery: storage, not a source. Hydrocarbons are roughly 18× denser per kilogram even after conversion losses. They add combustion plant, liquid logistics, carbon accounting and a mass that changes as it burns. They are the emergency and remote fallback, not the primary B.C. chain.

Status

A systems study, complete on its own terms

What exists: the module standard, the exchange geometry, the depot inventory arithmetic by Little's Law, and the full bill priced against BC Hydro's published 2026 tariff. What does not exist yet: engineered modules, sited depots, grid allocations, and a covering standard. The sim names the mechanism and models nothing beyond it: "Fleet throughput depends on battery tender ships ferrying charged cells for discharged ones, swapped at mechanical speed. Named as the mechanism, deliberately unmodelled beyond that."

  • Onboard generators are counted for thrust only. Crediting them as an energy source would move these numbers in the chain's favour; it is tracked as an open item.
  • The architecture sits outside every standard that exists. IEC 62840 covers road vehicles, and NEAT is a 12 MW-class testbed. The covering standard is part of the work rather than a box to tick.
  • Bounded battery-fire failure must be demonstrated at cell, module, magazine, vehicle, and depot scale, never assumed from modularity.
  • The numbers above are computed or cited, and the demand figures are superseded as marked: BC Hydro's RS 1830 tariff effective 2026-04-01, NREL round-trip modelling, NASA pack-level specific energy, and the published fleet model. None of it has been built, bought or connected. It is here so the size of the thing can be seen before anyone spends a dollar on it.
