1 · The parts

in 3D: a connector · a tube · the film

Three materials build everything: titanium printed into clamshell joints that clamp around their members, and carbon fibre pulled into stiff thin-walled tube. A tough polymer film forms into shallow domes before it ever meets a frame. Nothing exotic is assumed. The joints are weighed as finished printed parts, the tube is a catalogue product, and the film budget is priced at a weight a fabric supplier will quote.

2 · The cell

in 3D: the bench cell · into its centre

The first thing this project builds is a vacuum cell that fits on a bench: a m frame of tubes and printed joints under one sealed skin, pumped down to prove the joints, the film and the seal. It weighs kg. At bench size the sky's squeeze is brutal relative to the air a small volume displaces — which is exactly why this article is built to measure the parts, not the float. Lift per litre is fixed; structure per litre falls as the vessel grows, so the bench article proves the joints, the film and the seal, and scale earns the lift. No cell floats — scale is the lever, and the ship is where it pays.

3 · The wall

in 3D: the wall, up close · one crossing, clamped

The ship's pressure barrier is one continuous film laid straight onto hoop rings, of them around the barrel, about half a metre apart, with straight bars running lengthwise at the same pitch. The film lies on that grid in roughly square panels, each dished gently inward by the sky. Behind the film everything is already vacuum, so there is nothing back there to seal: no cells, no inner bags, one membrane doing one job.

A square panel matters more than it looks. Film curved in two directions carries pressure at half the tension of a long trough, which halves the film weight and halves the pull it hands to the frame. The panels' pulls gather in the rings, and the rings turn tens of thousands of small tugs into one hoop of compression around the barrel — a keystone arch closed on itself, carrying the load around the emptiness.

4 · The skeleton

in 3D: the grid, on the ship · through the wall

The film kills the atmosphere locally; it cannot keep a 52 metre hull round. That job belongs to a deep two-walled truss: heavy carbon chords in an outer and an inner wall about m apart, laced by diagonal webs, with pretensioned spokes crossing the void beyond. The depth is the strength — a deep sandwich resists bending the way an I-beam does, from the space between its flanges — and the sealed film wraps outside all of it, pressed on by the sky, so every strut in the ship stands in vacuum and counts toward lift. Nothing cuts through the wall.

5 · The hull

in 3D: the hull, whole · inside the void

Closed and pumped down, the hull is by metres and displaces m³ of air, which is worth tonnes of lift at sea level. Lift is fixed by geometry. Everything after that is a fight to keep structure under it, and this project publishes the fight rather than the trailer: the sky sets a floor on structure (build lighter and the hull crushes), the displaced air sets a ceiling (build heavier and it never leaves the ground), and the design must land between them.

In the best defensible world the corridor opens, and the current drawing sits a few tonnes off it on a two-thousand-tonne ledger. In the house-harsh world the floor rises above the ceiling at this size. That is the question the knockdown campaign exists to settle. The moves that close it are known and priced: better spoke geometry, tighter frames. None of them requires a material that does not exist. That margin, not a render, is what this project is building toward.

6 · The gear

in 3D: the ship, dressed · the water gear

Everything that is not lift hangs outside the vacuum, on a pipe-frame raft slung under the keel from a wide bridle: water tanks, nitrogen ballast, generators, batteries, the cryo plant, and the ship's mind. The working lines drop from the raft, and they are colour-coded by job — pink carries weight (the bucket line, the bridle), blue carries water (the pump's rigid pipe, the sprayer feed). The top half of the hull is decked in solar panels; the rotors sit on pylons on the horizontal beam of the ship, as far from lines and film as the geometry allows, because their hardest work is pushing up — holding the buoyant hull down against a lake while the pump drinks.

7 · At work

The mission is water: initial attack on a fire close to a lake, tanks filled on the winch while the rotors hold the ship down, drops on the head of the fire through the night while the crewed fleet sleeps. A live simulation flies the whole fleet against today's actual BC wildfires, on real terrain and real water. The crush arithmetic above closes near 96 m of hull, and two of the three classes it flies live outside that. They fly there to find where the arithmetic breaks, and the roster says so out loud.

What closes next: four items, priced

  • The residual. As drawn, the ship misses neutral buoyancy by t in the best defensible world, and by much more on the harsh basis. The priced path to closing it is spoke geometry and frame detail, not new physics. The residual is published on every page, and it moves the moment the model does.
  • The film's two-way credit. The wall is priced one-way today. When the drape analysis licenses the two-way rate, the wall gets lighter. The ledger does not yet book that upside.
  • Compartments. A tear should cost a compartment, not the ship. Compartment membranes across the void are the rule; choosing the arrangement is the open bulkhead study.
  • Scale limits. Bigger is not free: the crush envelope closes both ends, and the two larger fleet classes stand outside it today. The front page's envelope figure is interactive, and dragging it shows where physics says no.
