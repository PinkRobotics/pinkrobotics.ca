Pink Robotics — Airships · The engineering

The arithmetic runs in public.

A vacuum airship would need a hull weighing less than its displaced air. Holding the shape at that mass is the unresolved problem. The 3D viewer spans parts, a bench article and hull studies; the mission fleet is simulated and has never flown. Model figures update through the page binder; the cap readings below are checked against generated records. Some hardware and load paths remain unpriced or undefined.

As drawn, the 52 m hull does not float. The record basis uses safety factor 1.2 against full sea-level pressure, knockdown 0.30 and 1,050 MPa chords. Its lift is 0.558 of its mass at sea level and 0.436 at the 2,500 m working altitude. The favourable basis assumes knockdown 0.65 and a 1,450 MPa carbon-laminate compressive ceiling, both unverified. Its lift/mass is 0.981 at sea level and 0.766 at 2,500 m, short by 4.3 t and 53.6 t respectively.

The bill and the drawing disagree in the end caps, in both directions. Across five readings, the favourable lift/mass runs from 0.751 to 0.998 at sea level and from 0.586 to 0.780 at 2,500 m. No reading reaches 1; none is a checked design. The hull's member census records 20 disagreements between drawing and bill.

Closing the float budget needs tested knockdowns and chord coupons, defined cap load paths, and a complete mass bill. The ledger prices chordal spokes and in-surface shear only as bounds; shear hardware remains unpriced. Confirming the favourable inputs alone would not close the altitude deficit.

1 · The parts

in 3D: a connector · a tube · the film

Three materials build everything: titanium printed into clamshell joints that clamp around their members, and carbon fibre pulled into stiff thin-walled tube. A tough polymer film forms into shallow domes before it ever meets a frame. Nothing exotic is assumed. Hull joint mass is an allowance; bench-joint mass comes from its mesh, and neither has been weighed or tested.

3 · The wall

in 3D: the wall, up close · one crossing, clamped

The ship's pressure barrier is one continuous film laid straight onto hoop rings, of them around the barrel, about half a metre apart, with straight bars running lengthwise at the same pitch. The film lies on that grid in roughly square panels, each dished gently inward by the sky. Behind the film everything is already vacuum, so there is nothing back there to seal: no cells, no inner bags, one membrane doing one job.

A square panel matters more than it looks. Film curved in two directions carries pressure at half the tension of a long trough, which halves the film weight and halves the pull it hands to the frame. The panels' pulls gather in the rings, and the rings turn tens of thousands of small tugs into one hoop of compression around the barrel — a keystone arch closed on itself, carrying the load around the emptiness.

4 · The skeleton

in 3D: the grid, on the ship · through the wall

The film kills the atmosphere locally; it cannot keep a 52 metre hull round. That job belongs to a deep two-walled truss: heavy carbon chords in an outer and an inner wall about m apart, laced by diagonal webs, with pretensioned spokes crossing the void beyond. The depth is the strength — a deep sandwich resists bending the way an I-beam does, from the space between its flanges — and the sealed film wraps outside all of it, pressed on by the sky, so every strut in the ship stands in vacuum and counts toward lift. Nothing cuts through the wall.

5 · The hull

in 3D: the hull, whole · inside the void

The hull is by metres and encloses m³. The air displaced gives tonnes of lift at sea level. The model's greedy sizing at safety factor 1 is a computed case, not a minimum mass or a design with operating margin.

The diagram compares sea-level lift with greedy sizing at safety factor 1. Neither sizing result is a minimum mass, and a positive gap between lift and either one does not establish a ship. The ledger's working-altitude comparison remains short.

Knockdown and coupon campaigns would test the favourable inputs, not remove the remaining mass deficit. Chordal spokes and in-surface shear are proposed credits; the shear system's own mass is unpriced. Both need defined load paths, joints and structural checks before they can support a design claim.

6 · The gear

in 3D: the ship, dressed · the water gear

The flight model assumes a hull that floats; no drawn hull does.

Everything that is not lift hangs outside the vacuum, on a pipe-frame raft slung under the keel from a wide bridle: water tanks, nitrogen ballast, generators, batteries, the cryo plant, and the ship's mind. The working lines drop from the raft, and they are colour-coded by job — pink carries weight (the bucket line, the bridle), blue carries water (the pump's rigid pipe, the sprayer feed). The top half of the hull is decked in solar panels; the rotors sit on pylons on the horizontal beam of the ship, as far from lines and film as the geometry allows, because their hardest work is pushing up — holding the buoyant hull down against a lake while the pump drinks.

What closes next: four items, priced

  • The residual. The two-altitude comparison above is the present float result. The ledger sets out the tests and design work still needed; neither proposed credit establishes a drawn hull.
  • The film's two-way credit. The wall is priced one-way today. When the drape analysis licenses the two-way rate, the wall gets lighter. The ledger does not yet book that upside.
  • Compartments. A tear should cost a compartment, not the ship. Compartment membranes across the void are the rule; choosing the arrangement is the open bulkhead study.
  • Scale limits. Bigger is not free: structural float depends on the stability basis, member strength and altitude. The float ledger and interactive front-page figure expose those assumptions.
