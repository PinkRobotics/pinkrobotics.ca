The vacuum cell

A vacuum airship lifts by holding nothing. The hard part has never been the lift — it is surviving the atmosphere that is trying to crush the nothing. This is how a hull made of many sealed cells changes that problem, what it solves, and the one thing it does not.

One number decides everything

A vacuum shell heavier than the air it displaces has no net lift at any size, at any scale, ever. Lighter, and the hull can always be grown until it closes. Everything below is about getting under this line.

This page argues the sealed-cell architecture, and the ship has moved past it — twice. The tiles retired the cells, and the rings retired the tiles: the current wall is one film laid straight on hoop rings, square half-metre panels, cross-bars one diameter outboard, on a two-walled carbon skeleton with tension spokes across the void. Behind that barrier everything is already vacuum, so nothing back there is sealed: no interior films, no per-cell pump-down. Breach containment is compartment membranes at a coarser pitch.

Three things survive here unchanged. The shared-wall arithmetic: an interior wall with vacuum on both sides carries nothing. The wall density that decides the whole project. And the sealed cell itself as the bench article, the coupon that proves clamps, films, seals and a months-long hold. See the blueprint and the ship in 3D.

Why cells, and not one big envelope

Every vacuum-balloon study in the literature analyses a single sphere, and reaches the same negative result: a shell thin enough to float buckles under one atmosphere. Akhmeteli & Gavrilin showed a perfect diamond sphere collapses at about 0.2 atm. The collapse is scale-free. No change of material engineers around it.

What that swaps. The array is no longer a shell. It is a solid body of cellular material in hydrostatic compression, and what governs is the crushing strength of a cellular solid at a relative density low enough to float. That is a hard problem. It is not an impossibility proof, and it is the problem an architected lattice is built to answer.

Struts slender past the point a lattice survives

To float, the solid fraction of the hull must be a few parts in ten thousand. At that density the members of any lattice are so thin that they buckle long before the material yields. Strength then scales as the square of density, not linearly, and the arithmetic collapses.

Unless the struts are hollow. A tube puts its material where the bending stiffness is. Sizing the tube so that Euler buckling and local wall buckling fail together changes the exponent from φ² to φ1.5. That change determines whether the vehicle is possible.

The scale ladder

The tube wall thickness is a fixed fraction of the strut length, about 1.8×10⁻⁴. That is a manufacturing instruction hiding inside a stress calculation: you cannot make the lattice small. A wall you can actually lay down sets a floor on how long the struts must be, and therefore on how big one cell is.

What one flooded cell costs its neighbours

In normal operation an interior wall carries nothing. Flood one cell and its shared walls have an atmosphere pushing into the vacuum next door. The neighbouring lattice reacts that on top of the global load. This local contingency sizes the interior structure because it is the only time the interior sees pressure.

Aerogel inside the struts

It is the obvious next idea. A core stabilises a tube wall against wrinkling, which is what a sandwich does, so the wall could be thinner. Against that, the core is mass carrying no load. Aerogel stiffness falls as roughly the cube of its density, so the two effects race each other.

Two levers that fail

null result

Partial vacuum does not help

Lift goes as (poutside − pinside). So does the load the shell carries. The ratio is unchanged, exactly. Running the cells at half a vacuum halves the lift and halves the load, and buys nothing at all.

null result

Altitude does not help either

The requirement is ρshell < ρair, and ρshell scales with pressure — so what matters is ρ/p = 1/RT, a function of temperature alone. Thin air lifts less in exactly the proportion it presses less hard. Cold air is the only free lunch, and it is a small one.

What is buildable this month

Nothing printable floats, and it is not close. A printed cell is still the experiment worth running, because it tests the model rather than the vehicle. It asks whether a cell of this architecture holds one atmosphere at the relative density predicted, and whether it fails where predicted.

The honest summary. The sealed-cell architecture is what turns an impossibility proof into an engineering problem, and hollow struts are what turn the engineering problem into a buildable one. Both conclusions are robust. What no consumer process reaches is the constituent material: the flight article needs continuous aerospace carbon, and a printer that lays chopped fibre is roughly an order of magnitude away from it.
