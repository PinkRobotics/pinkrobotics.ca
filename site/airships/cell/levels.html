Pink Robotics — Airships · The build, staged

No drawn hull floats.
The ship is the goal.

For a season the question here was how to make one vacuum cell lighter than the air it displaces. The answer is that it never needs to be. The bench cell stays over the wall on purpose. A cell is the bench article. A ship is a different machine: one film laid straight on hoop rings, a deep two-walled skeleton carrying the sky's whole squeeze. Tension spokes cross the void, and a core of engineered nothing does the lifting. Seven levels, from sintered titanium and rolled carbon to a working ship on a fire line. This page is the blueprint the explorer follows.

As drawn, the 52 m hull does not float. The record basis uses structural safety factor 1.2 against full sea-level pressure, knockdown 0.30 and 1,050 MPa chords. Its lift is 0.558 of its mass at sea level and 0.436 at 2,500 m. The favourable basis assumes knockdown 0.65 and a 1,450 MPa carbon-laminate compressive ceiling, both unverified, at the same safety factor. Its lift-to-mass ratios are 0.981 at sea level and 0.766 at 2,500 m: short by 4.3 t and 53.6 t respectively.

Closing the gap needs knockdown tests, chord coupons and a complete mass bill. The chordal net and in-surface shear credits are bounds, with geometry or hardware still unpriced; neither establishes a drawn floating hull.

The bill and the drawing disagree in the end caps, in both directions. Across five readings the favourable lift-to-mass ratio spans 0.751–0.998 at sea level and 0.586–0.780 at 2,500 m. No reading reaches 1; none is a checked design.

The drawing and the bill disagree in 20 places, recorded for the float ledger in the member census.

Level 2 · The cell

in 3D: the cell · into the centre

One Kelvin cell: the first buildable block, deliberately not lighter than air. It assembles from the catalog's parts. Tubes are cut to a measured saw table, titanium clamps close around every meeting of members, and the formed film is drawn over the frame. It then takes its vacuum and proves every joint and film load the ship will reuse a hundred thousand times.

Level 3 · The band

in 3D: the wall, up close · the whole hull

The band is the ship's pressure barrier and nothing more: one layer of film, carried on the skeleton's own hoop chords, closing the whole vehicle against an atmosphere. It is not made of cells. Behind it everything is already vacuum, so there is nothing back there to seal. No interior films, no per-cell pump-down, no vessel walls doing a job the barrier has already done.

What breaks the film into panels is the structure it lies on. Hoop chords run round the barrel about half a metre apart, and straight cross-bars run along it at the same pitch. The film lies on them in square panels, dished inward under the sky. Square matters: a doubly-curved panel carries pressure in both directions at half the tension of a long trough. That halves the film, and halves the pull it puts on the frame. Nothing is drilled, cut, or woven. Both members run continuous, and a clamp holds them every couple of metres.

Redundancy comes later and it comes as compartments: bulkhead membranes at a coarser pitch, so a tear costs a compartment rather than the ship. That study is open, and the page says so rather than implying the wall is already fault-tolerant. The order is deliberate: make it work, then armour it.

Level 4 · The grid & skins

in 3D: the grid, on the ship · one crossing, clamped · from the ship

The big struts arrive. The band can kill the atmosphere locally but cannot carry the ship's global squeeze. That belongs to a deep sandwich shell: two faces of fat carbon chord pipe held metres apart by web diagonals, joined by the same clamped titanium joints as the cell, only larger. The hoop chords are the ring frames. The sealed band wraps just outside the outer face, pressed onto it by the sky. A weather jacket closes over it, and a thin void skin bounds the core within.

The interplay is a clean division of labour. Follow the load: the sky dishes each half-metre panel of film inward, and the panel hands its pull to the rings it lies on; the rings turn roughly seventy thousand small pulls into one great hoop of compression around the barrel; the webs carry shear between the walls; the inner wall's longerons carry the caps' whole thrust down the length. Nothing ever pushes back from inside the void: the near side of the hull is balanced by the far side, the load carried around the emptiness by the rings as hoop compression — a keystone arch, closed on itself. The sandwich's metres of depth are empty truss on purpose — that gap is where the shell earns its bending stiffness, the way an I-beam earns its from the space between flanges — and the second review round added the member that makes the gap real: crossed diagonals in every ring plane, because the meridional fan cannot carry ring-plane shear, and without ring-plane shear two walls are just two walls.

Across the void itself run the spokes: pretensioned tension cords, the ruled exception to nothing-crosses-the-void. They are a foundation rather than a frame: they resist the even-numbered out-of-round modes at fibre weight. The record says exactly what they cannot do: a diametral cord has no first-order stiffness against the odd modes. The checks page carries that finding, and SHIP-3 owes the chordal layout that would.

Seen in section, the ship is skin and bones. The bones are a single endoskeleton: a two-walled truss, heavy chords in both walls laced by the webs. A single-walled ring would have no depth to resist going out-of-round. The skin is one membrane wrapped around it, pressed onto the bones by the sky itself. Outside, the ship is small, light and delicate. The inside is heavy: big, strong members, and there is nothing in the middle. And the boundary sits at the largest radius the ship owns. Everything inboard lives in vacuum and counts as lift: webs, both walls, the whole depth of the machine.

The junction forced this arrangement. Webs must lace the two truss walls, and a sealed wall dislikes being crossed. The ruling puts the wall outside everything it would otherwise have to dodge. Nothing penetrates it. Every strut lives behind it, in the lift. The loaded membrane faces nothing but sky. Every attachment lands on the unloaded inner side, at film corners that already terminate sealed on their joints.

Level 6 · Equipment & paint

Rotors, tanks, avionics, the winch, the livery: everything the residual lift exists to buy. On the ledger these are named lines held open on purpose: the payload axis. The design rule stays what it has always been here: every line named, none silently zero. The paint goes on last, on the one skin that never feels the atmosphere.

equipment budget: none on either basis at either altitude; the hull is already short of lift equipment lines: named, not yet weighed
